Attic cleanouts, and what has been up there since the last owner

By the Hero’s team. Updated September 2026. A 7 minute read.

Most attic cleanouts are not about volume. They are about access. Everything has to come down a pull-down ladder or through a hatch, in a space where you cannot walk anywhere except on the joists, in heat that runs well past whatever the thermostat downstairs says. We bring it down. You point.

The other thing about attics is that a good share of what is up there was not put there by you.

Why an attic is harder than a garage

A garage cleanout is a sorting job. You can see everything, you can stand up, and you can carry a box out a door at ground level. An attic is none of that.

You can only step where there is decking or a joist. In a lot of North Texas houses the decking covers maybe a third of the attic and the rest is loose insulation lying over drywall, which will not hold a person. A foot in the wrong place comes through a bedroom ceiling. That is the most common way an attic cleanout turns into a repair bill, and it usually happens to a homeowner doing it alone on a Saturday.

Then there is the ladder. Everything that comes down goes down a folding wooden or aluminum ladder with a person on it holding a box. Pull-down ladders carry a rated capacity, and that rating counts the person and whatever they are carrying together. Two people, one on the ladder and one at the bottom, is not a nicety up there.

And it is hot. An attic under a dark roof in July is a genuinely bad place to make decisions, which is why we schedule attic jobs for the morning when we can.

What is actually up there

Attic contents sort into about four groups, and they get handled differently.

Yours, and you want it. Christmas bins, luggage, a crib, the boxes of photos. This is the group people are actually afraid of losing, so it comes down first and goes in a spot you pick before anything else moves.

Yours, and it has been up there eleven years. A broken box fan, the exercise bike, curtain rods from a house you sold, boxes nobody has opened since the move. This is usually most of the pile.

The last owner's. Nearly every house we clear has some of this. Leftover flooring and tile, cut-down carpet, storm windows, a satellite dish, sometimes furniture that was carried up and then could not be carried back down. None of it is yours and none of it is worth keeping.

Building material that came with the house. Insulation, ductwork scraps, wiring offcuts. Some of it matters and some of it is trash, and telling the difference is not something to guess at from the hatch.

The sorting rule that works here is the same one that works in a garage. Touch a thing once, and decide about it while it is in your hand. The longer version of that method is in the garage cleanout checklist, and it holds up just as well ten feet off the ground.

Do not start dragging things toward the hatch before we get there. People do this to save time, and it usually ends with a stack of boxes sitting on unsupported drywall right where the crew needs to stand. Leave it where it sits. Getting it to the hatch is the part we are there to do.

Three things we will not just carry down

The attic is the one part of a house where a cleanout can run into something that stops the job, and we would rather say so up front than find out with a box already in somebody's hands.

Loose grey or silver insulation that looks like small pebbles. In houses built before the early 1990s that can be vermiculite, and some vermiculite is contaminated with asbestos. The correct move is to leave it alone entirely and not disturb it. The EPA guidance on vermiculite insulation is worth reading before anybody goes up there, us included. We are haulers, not an abatement company, and we will say so rather than stir it up.

A roof leak that has been quiet for a while. Stained decking, damp insulation, and the smell that goes with it. Wet material carried down through the house is how a contained problem gets spread into the rest of it. The EPA has a plain homeowner page on mold cleanup that covers where the do-it-yourself line sits. Get the roof handled first, then call us for the debris.

Old paint, solvents, and pesticide left behind by whoever lived there before. These are household hazardous waste and they do not go on our truck at all. Most cities in the northern suburbs take them free from residents. Plano runs a household chemical collection program and the neighboring cities run their own. We will point you at yours rather than guess at it.

The rest of what we do and do not take is spelled out in what junk removal will and will not take.

Where it goes once it is down

Very little out of an attic is a donation candidate, and it is worth being honest about that instead of implying otherwise. Heat, dust, and years of storage are hard on furniture and fabric, and most donation centers turn down anything stained, warped, smoke damaged, or missing hardware. Habitat for Humanity ReStore publishes what its locations accept on its donate goods page, and it varies store to store.

What is genuinely usable goes to local donation partners. Metal, including old ductwork, bed frames, and that satellite dish, goes to a metal recycler. Clean cardboard has its own destination. Our stated goal is diverting more than half of every load away from a landfill, and separating on the way out is how that gets done rather than promised. The full walkthrough is in where your junk goes after pickup.

If a few large items are all you have, check the free route first. Most cities here run bulky waste collection on a published schedule, and Plano lists exactly what qualifies at bulky waste accepted items. The catch is that you still have to get it from the attic to the curb yourself, which is the part that sends most people looking for help in the first place. We would rather tell you the free option exists and lose the job than take money you did not have to spend.

How an attic cleanout runs

You send photos of the hatch and whatever you can see from it. A crew comes out, looks at the access and the decking, and gives you a firm number before anything is lifted, with disposal already included. The quote is the bill.

Then two people work it, one in the attic and one at the bottom of the ladder. You do not go up, you do not carry, and the landing spot gets swept before we leave. Our own crew does the work, not gig labor hired off an app that morning, which is the reason we are comfortable sending people into the top of your house at all. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to bring things down to the garage first?

No. Carrying it down is the job. If you have already moved some of it that is fine, but nothing about the quote assumes you did any of the lifting.

What if I do not know what is up there?

That is normal, especially if you bought the house with the attic already full. A crew looks at it on site and tells you what it is before anything gets decided.

Will you go up if the only access is a pull-down ladder?

Usually yes. What matters more is the condition of the ladder and how much decking there is to stand on. Send a photo of both and we will tell you straight.

Can you take the old insulation too?

Sometimes, and only after somebody has confirmed what it is. Anything that might be vermiculite is a hard stop for us and belongs with a licensed abatement contractor instead.

How long does an attic take?

Less time than people expect once two people are working it, because the ladder trips are the whole job and they move much faster with someone at the bottom.

The attic is the last part of the house anybody deals with, which is exactly why the stuff up there has outlasted two owners. The rest of what we haul is on the services page. There is no rush on our end.
